# Intro

From May 12 to May 21, the Mechatronics and Robotics Society (MARS) at the 
University of Virginia competed in the 2026 Lunabotics Challenge at the 
University of Central Florida (UCF) and Kennedy Space Center (KSC). We are 
beyond excited to share that MARS [won first place overall](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/kennedy/nasas-2026-lunabotics-winning-student-teams-engineering-lunar-future/)
taking home the Artemis Off World Grand Prize and five additional awards.

The [NASA Lunabotics Challenge]("https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/lunabotics-challenge/")
is an annual competition where university teams design and build robots capable 
of excavating and transporting lunar regolith (lunar dirt). Robots have to 
excavate lunar regolith, carry it across a crater- and boulder-laden obstacle 
zone, and align in a construction zone to build a berm. Robots are scored by 
how much they build normalized to their mass and energy consumed. Additional 
points are granted for differing levels of autonomy implemented in the 
competition run, communication bandwidth, and consideration of dust in the 
design and operation.

The Lunabotics Challenge is aligned with NASA’s [Artemis Program](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/artemis/) 
and its recently announced [Moon Base](https://www.nasa.gov/moonbase/) 
informing how NASA will create a sustainable human presence on the Moon. 
Berms on the Moon, similar to the ones built in the competition, can be used 
for protection against rocket ejecta around launch and landing pads, shielding 
for cryogenics, and radiation protection for human habitats.

The robotic construction challenge consisted of qualifiers at UCF from May 12 
to May 17 where teams had the opportunity to complete a 10 minute practice run 
and two 15 minute qualifying runs. The top 10 teams moved onto the finals at 
KSC from May 19 to May 21 and competed in one 30 minute run and one 20 minute 
run (shortened from 30 minutes due to time constraints).

In addition to robotic construction, the Lunabotics Challenge has three other 
deliverables comprising the total score: a report on how systems engineering 
was utilized throughout the robot design process, a presentation and 
demonstration of the robot to NASA and industry professionals, and a report 
on engagement with local STEM companies and community leaders.

Over the past nine months, our student-run team of 105 members worked hard 
to design, fabricate, program, and test our robot for this competition. Every 
year, we start from the drawing board analyzing NASA’s guidebook and devising 
how we want to win the competition.

This year, due to our large member size, we were able to take two completely 
different approaches to our robot design. We developed two different robot 
systems in parallel throughout the year, fully constructed each, and tested 
them in competition simulations to determine which was the best to bring to 
competition. From that process, we determined the one-robot system was the 
robot we should bring to the competition.

On May 10, we performed final tests on the robot, disassembled it for transport, 
and packed it away for the 800 mile drive from Charlottesville to Orlando. 
On May 11 early in the morning, we departed for competition.

# UCF

### First team in pits, first practice run

Early in the morning on May 12, we unloaded our robot, Bruno, and all our tools 
at the UCF pits, where teams have a space to prepare their robots for competition 
runs. Arriving at UCF an hour before check-ins began, we were the first team 
there giving us ample time to prepare our robot before practice runs even began. 
In the two days prior to the start of official qualifying runs at UCF, teams are 
given the opportunity to have a practice run. Last year, we were the first team 
to have a practice run due to our level of preparation. Coming into UCF this 
year, we aimed to repeat being the first team to have a practice run, and we were 
able to do just that. We signed up for the first practice run time slot as soon as 
we were able to, and once the time was confirmed we began a thorough inspection 
of our robot, Bruno, ensuring that all features would work as intended in the UCF 
Exolith Arena.

When it was time for our [practice run](https://www.youtube.com/live/MpGzeJDk9-w?si=GVHJV2O8QUTl2_sk&t=1923), 
we were fully ready and confident about Bruno’s performance. We were prepared in 
every way for this practice run with check lists for everyone involved in the run, 
including the mission control personnel controlling Bruno and the arena personnel 
in charge of setting up Bruno inside the arena. During our practice run, Bruno 
operated as expected, and we were able to test all features of our plan for the 
official competition runs, including autonomous actions and a unique driving method 
that we call drum drive: traversing the arena using the excavation drums rather 
than the wheels of the robot.

### Runs at UCF

Our [first run](https://www.youtube.com/live/bnHTBy5lAvI?si=Mf2ft-2CaRCzvfks&t=18717) 
at UCF took place on May 14 and our [second run](https://www.youtube.com/live/KpqKq_c-gao?si=PfJO0L0mx2XsGxL_&t=21168) 
took place on May 16. Building off the lessons learned during our practice run, 
both of our UCF runs went largely according to plan. The one notable hiccup 
occurred halfway into both of our runs during the dumping operation, when our 
robot accidentally tipped over. Tipping had always been a major risk going into 
competition, given the imbalance created when dumping one side of our robot at 
a time. To mitigate this, we had practiced tipping recovery extensively before 
competition. That preparation paid off: when our robot tipped mid-run, our 
driver recovered quickly, allowing us to continue building large berms without 
significant time loss.

Competing in a lunar regolith simulant at UCF also taught us a great deal about 
our robot's capabilities. The robot’s skid steer drivetrain handled wonderfully, 
exceeding our expectations based on testing in sand volleyball courts at UVA. We 
had also been concerned that our drums might not fill to designed capacity in 
regolith since the material could potentially flow out or clog prematurely. 
Instead, the drums again exceeded expectations, and we found that regolith can 
fill them well above our 50% designed capacity. Out of caution around tipping, 
however, we tried to avoid overfilling the drums, so their true maximum fill 
capacity remains to be determined.

### UCF Results

After two solid runs and sizable berms built, we were confident that we had a 
slot in the top 10 locked up. On top of our berm-building abilities, we were also 
one of the lightest robots at the competition and were able to successfully 
execute all of our planned autonomy routines — both huge advantages in point 
scoring. Given these metrics, we knew we would be somewhere near the top, but we 
couldn’t be sure just how well we shaped up to the rest of the competition until 
the awards ceremony.

At the awards ceremony, the first award we received was a superlative for 
building the largest berm of any team in a competition run. Next, we learned that 
we scored the third most autonomy points in the qualifiers despite it being the 
first time we had ever attempted autonomous routines in our history. With these 
two victories already disclosed, it was a surprise to no one when we were 
announced as the number one scoring team, and the first to book their tickets to 
the finals at KSC.

We didn’t know it at the time, but we had just put up an unprecedented 
performance at UCF. When detailed scoring breakdowns were released later, we 
learned just how far ahead of the competition we were. Our two runs totaled to a 
combined score of 3703 points, with second place being a staggering 1200 points 
behind us. For perspective, the previous year’s UCF winner scored a total of 
around 1800 points, a metric that we topped with just our first run alone, which 
scored over 2000 points. It was safe to say we were heavy favorites coming into 
KSC.

# KSC

### Run 1: A wheel falls off

We had our [first run](https://www.youtube.com/live/6TjQcgX_XBo?si=Hxl0RhiujX012F37&t=19402) 
at KSC on May 19, and we were the second team to have a competition run at the finals. 
In preparation for our first run at KSC, the team performed our standard inspection 
procedure. Having completed these steps in UCF multiple times, we moved through 
inspection smoothly and felt ready for our first run at finals. After the mission 
control and arena personnel went into their designated locations, the rest of the 
team stood together to watch our first run at the KSC finals.

Relative to how smoothly things went at UCF, our run started out rough. On our 
first cycle, we accidentally ran our dump autonomy script with the wrong 
direction selected, costing us time and  half of that cycle’s material. A couple 
cycles later, our first attempt at traversal autonomy failed, and we drove 
directly into a rock. There were no damages to the robot, but we were struggling 
to get into the groove that let our robot berm build at its best. The KSC arena 
was proving incredibly hostile — a much denser obstacle field and a more 
challenging regolith simulant made maneuvering around the arena much less 
consistent and reliable.

While the run wasn’t going as perfectly as we had hoped, we had no idea how much 
worse it was going to get. 12 minutes into the run, as we were making a turn to 
line up for our second attempt at traversal autonomy, one of our wheels completely 
broke off of the robot — a run ending failure. Our hearts dropped as we watched 
our hopes of winning, everything we worked towards for the whole year, disappear 
in an instant.

However, our members in mission control were not discouraged, and with seemingly 
nothing left to lose, decided to continue with the rest of the run with only 
three wheels. In order to drive while missing a wheel, we lowered our bucket 
drums into the regolith surface, lifting the remaining three wheels from the 
surface, and began traversing in the regolith with the drums spinning. This was a 
method of traversal we have practiced before during testing sessions at the UVA 
sand volleyball courts as well as in our UCF practice run. Therefore, we knew 
beforehand that this alternative traversal method was an option for us in case it 
was needed in a run. This drum traversal was very effective at moving in one 
direction quickly, and garnered many cheers from the other teams watching our run. 
In addition to drum traversal, we attempted driving and turning with only three 
wheels. Despite never testing these maneuvers before, we found them to work well 
enough to help us complete three more dump cycles in the remaining time of our run.

As the arena crew carried Bruno back into the pits, we were met with applause and 
cheers from the crowd as other teams came up to us and commended us on the run.

### Run 2: Comeback

On May 20, we had our [second run](https://www.youtube.com/live/wdADLct6T7c?si=GuHnFkhxOYG-Gcas&t=28973) 
at KSC. We were incredibly nervous in anticipation of this run as we knew that it 
had to be near perfect in order to offset the points we lost in our first KSC run. 
This second run would determine our placement in the berm construction category and 
therefore our overall placement in the competition. Although a wheel fell off in our 
first KSC run, we knew that we still had a chance to place in the top three of the 
competition, and possibly even first place. Despite operating with only three wheels 
for the majority of our first run, we still built a sizable berm that even exceeded 
some of the other teams.

Based on the near catastrophic event of our first run, we had a plan to ensure 
that our second KSC run would go without a major hiccup. Anticipating any issues 
that may happen, we brought many backup wheels and drums to the competition. 
Because of this, we decided as a team to switch out our plastic wheels with 
backup metal wheels. We had a group of mechanical members come into the KSC pits 
early in the morning to ensure we had enough time to switch out our plastic 
wheels to the metal wheels along with the general inspection checks we perform 
prior to a run.

### Awards Ceremony

We won 6 awards at KSC, the most of any team at competition.

On Wednesday, May 20th, we won [2nd place in systems engineering](https://www.youtube.com/live/wdADLct6T7c?si=ZFHKC6ROJd-EMVJX&t=17495).

During the lunch break on Thursday, May 21st, we received the [Innovation Award](https://www.youtube.com/live/yyLjc4PaIlw?si=dAEAk9_qVCOvSqH_&t=15070), 
presented by Rob Mueller. Mueller set the stage by telling the crowd that this 
award is rooted in doing something that has never been done before, and that NASA 
wanted to learn something from the winner. He described how this year, one team 
took the IPEx robot — a double-bucket drum mining robot going to the Moon in 2029 
which NASA thought it knew very well — and innovated with our novel drum design, 
reimagining a subject that had been deeply studied by the space agency for 
decades. Mueller went on to our perseverance after one of our wheels broke off 
the chassis: “The second thing they did is they failed. They failed badly.” But 
rather than stopping with three wheels, we kept going. As he put it, dozens of 
teams would have walked away after a failure like that, but we refused. And 
through it all, he noted, we were laughing, wearing silly hats, and having a good 
time. Mueller called that spirit the third pillar of true innovation: staying 
relaxed, maintaining a good attitude, and holding the team together when things 
fall apart.

During the awards ceremony on Thursday, May 21st, we won four more awards. The 
University of Virginia was featured at every segment of the awards ceremony and 
were constantly called to the stage.  

We won [2nd place in autonomy](https://www.youtube.com/live/yyLjc4PaIlw?si=OWTsgmrXbpfiNOBJ&t=32472), 
presented by Caterpillar, which came with a certain irony we couldn’t help but 
appreciate. Our “autonomy” had no intelligence at all. It was simply a combination 
of predetermined values and recorded inputs played back during our runs, with no 
adaptive decision-making whatsoever. What we did have was a thorough reading of the 
rulebook; we had dissected the scoring criteria well enough and chose a path that 
maximized our points while minimizing effort and risk. The fact that this approach 
earned us 2nd place in an autonomy category says less about our robot's intelligence 
and more about having a reliable system.

Before the grand prize was announced, we received a [Special Recognition for Mission Excellence](https://www.youtube.com/live/yyLjc4PaIlw?si=dhXS7tPSQ-np5vq-&t=32628), 
presented by Rebecca Mazzone, who spoke about how we genuinely stood out during 
our time in the Mission Control Center. She described a team that was positive, 
professional, calm, and cohesive across every scenario, one that communicated 
effectively and presented what she called “genuine unity.” She added that this 
team seemed to enjoy being in it together, and that their resilience under 
pressure was exactly the kind of quality NASA looks for in its young engineers.

Afterwards, we placed [2nd in berm construction](https://www.youtube.com/live/yyLjc4PaIlw?si=V4BIFzg6TycoPtiY&t=33355), 
which was shocking considering the fact that we had lost a wheel in our first run, 
which severely hampered our ability to construct berms. 

Saving the best for last, we won the [Artemis Off World Grand Prize](https://www.youtube.com/live/yyLjc4PaIlw?si=XpGNIfdECQTi_Kla&t=33470). 
Mueller described the honor simply: it is a combination of everything, and it 
goes not to the team with the biggest berm, but to the team with the best system.

# Non-Robot Activities at Competition  

While winning Lunabotics took a lot of hard work from everyone, we also found 
plenty of time for some fun activities.

### CAT Command

One of the staples of Lunabotics is the CAT Command challenge, where participants 
are tasked with remotely teleoperating a CAT vehicle through a designated course 
as fast as possible. While most teams take this as a light challenge for fun, for 
us, it’s just as important as the main competition. To maximize our chances of 
success, we had as many people as we could attempt the challenge, as well as 
watching each other’s runs to get familiar with the course and glean optimal 
strategies. At UCF, we had a total of 20 members complete the challenge, which 
was the most out of any school at the competition. We also partook in CAT’s social 
media challenge, where our Instagram reel highlighting our experience with the 
challenge garnered the second most likes out of any school, and was the most 
appreciated by the CAT judges. Despite our best efforts and chalance, none of our 
members were able to score a time within the top three, with our best member being 
only 8 seconds away from a podium finish.

At KSC, a new CAT Command challenge appeared, giving us a chance to redeem our 
lackluster performance at UCF. While our quantity of participants took a slight 
dip, our quality skyrocketed. We had 14 members attempt the challenge, still the 
most out of any school, and completely [swept the fastest times leaderboard](https://www.youtube.com/live/yyLjc4PaIlw?si=jgWcAX_70FW7FwuG&t=31669). 
Our members occupied first, second, third, fourth, and sixth place at competition, 
with times that rivaled even the trained CAT operators themselves.

Our winning didn’t end there, however. At the UCF awards ceremony, CAT showed off 
the music video of their rap song “Dozer Pride”, which was met with overwhelmingly 
positive reception from the crowd. In response, CAT put out a challenge to teams 
to learn the dozer rap and perform it for them live at KSC. With a new challenge 
to conquer, we sprang into action, and listened to nothing but dozer rap on repeat 
for the next few days. We hosted rehearsals until every single one of our members 
knew the rap by heart. Once we were ready, we gave a live performance to the 
harbinger of the challenge, Eric Reiner, on his birthday nonetheless. Needless to 
say it was a smashing success, and as the only team that took the challenge 
seriously, we were named the undisputed champions of [dozer rap](https://www.youtube.com/live/yyLjc4PaIlw?si=lgGK2-Nae3-M0DCB&t=31830).

For our efforts in both CAT command and dozer rap, we were awarded the entire 
spoils of CAT merchandise, and left KSC dripped out in CAT branded safety glasses, 
caps, and backpacks.

### UCF Mixer

Another big portion of our team’s time, particularly at UCF, was spent talking to 
and scouting other teams and their robots. Members gained insights into how other 
teams approached the challenge and learned about the different designs that each 
team had. While we learned a lot of valuable information that will help inform 
and motivate our design decisions in the future, we also began to form more 
personal connections with other teams. 

Following the UCF awards ceremony, we hosted a dinner at a nearby plaza with 8 
different teams and more than 80 students in attendance.
